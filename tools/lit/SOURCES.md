## Section 1 — "Earliest Films and the Silent Era" (teacher's Google Slides, 17 slides)
1. Title: Earliest Films and the Silent Era (posters: Phantom of the Opera, Keaton's The Cameraman, A Trip to the Moon, Pearl White in The Thief, Chaplin's The Kid)
2. Early inventions in moving image: optical toys (flip book, thaumatrope, zoetrope) give the illusion of movement → first moving image → first film (late 1800s)
3. Capturing moving image: first recorded moving images used by scientists; 1882 Étienne-Jules Marey's chronophotographic gun to study body movement
4. Thomas Edison: Kinetograph (1888), photographs quickly one after another on a strip of film; watched through a peephole machine, the Kinetoscope
5. Fred Ott's Sneeze (Edison Kinetoscopic Record of a Sneeze, Jan 7, 1894)
6. Kinetoscope debuted 1893 Chicago World's Fair; Fred Ott's Sneeze first copyrighted film (1894); Robert William Paul film projector; 1895/96 Edison Vitascope ("Edison's Greatest Marvel")
7. Nickelodeons: amusement parks, carnivals, storefronts; individual machines like an arcade; a nickel; ~1 minute; later screens with pianos and organs; everyday activities, sports, misc.
8. Lumière brothers 1895: Auguste and Louis, Cinematograph = camera + projector, projected on screen, many viewers at once
9. Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat (1895): legend that audiences screamed and ran to the back
10. Georges Méliès: illusionist/magician; discovered special-effects editing when camera froze; A Trip to the Moon — first science fiction film; loosely based on Jules Verne
11. A Trip to the Moon (1902) video
12. Silent era: one-reelers 10–15 min; feature films started in Australia; D.W. Griffith's Birth of a Nation (1915), most popular film of 1910s, 3 hours, ended one-reelers; extremely racist and inspired hate groups
13. Silent era cont.: New York (Queens) led early filmmaking; 1920s Hollywood world leader; ~800 films a year; Chaplin, Keaton, Tom Mix, Douglas Fairbanks, Clara Bow
14. Charlie Chaplin in The Circus (clip)
15. Features of a silent movie: over-acting (body language, facial expression); live music (piano/organ) for emotional cues and mood; intertitles ("subtitles") between scenes for setting, plot, dialogue
16. Sound — talkies: Warner Bros synchronized sound; The Jazz Singer (1927) first "talkie", mostly silent with patches of dialogue and four songs; first all-talking feature The Lights of New York (1928)
17. "Go forth and take your quiz!"

Note to fact-check when writing: slide 6 says Vitascope 1895 (public debut April 1896); slide 10 spells "Milies" (Méliès); "subtitles" = intertitles.

## Reading — "A Very Short History of the Transition from Silent to Sound Movies", Emily Thompson (© 2011; written as a companion to Wonderstruck)
Google Doc 16p3wp1jXR8oRSYlh4HHPu-Q22pjQ8ZxwCxIWcRyEDZ4. Copyrighted: use facts only, never its sentences.
- 1877 Edison phonograph: horn → needle cuts wavy groove in soft wax; playback rides the groove
- 1890s Edison movies; wanted phonograph + film = "illusion of life"; failed: hard to sync, recordings too quiet
- Silent films: titles (intertitles) for dialogue; actors' faces and actions; Chaplin's Little Tramp never spoke
- Theater musicians in a pit played to the mood; deaf viewers (Rose in Wonderstruck) followed by acting + titles; 1920s people went several times a week
- Telephone-company inventors: electric microphones + amplifiers → loud enough, easier sync; Hollywood: "Who needs sound?"
- Warner brothers (Harry, Al, Sam, Jack): first theater Pennsylvania 1903; 1925 Sam (radio fan, built a radio station) pushes sound
- Plan: still silent films, but recorded orchestra replaces live musicians; studio picks the music; same everywhere
- Vitaphone ("the sound of life"); first: Don Juan (1926) — orchestra + synced sound effects (swords, bells); rivals: "just a fad"
- Shorts with singers/comedians; 1927 The Jazz Singer, Al Jolson talks with his "mother" → audiences want talk
- Hollywood's costly switch: equipment, soundproof stages, actors with bad voices/accents replaced, memorize lines, stand still near mics, directors can't shout, cameras in hot soundproof boxes ("turkeys roasting"), cameras can't move
- Projectionists run phonograph + projector; needle skips → lost sync → booing; theater musicians lose jobs; a few studio musicians heard nationwide
- Audiences must be quiet; deaf/hard-of-hearing struggle (no titles, actors turn away, less body language); some headphones; Edison was hard of hearing; "talkies" = static talking scenes
- By 1930: quiet cameras, better mics, sound-on-film (optical soundtrack of light/dark beside the pictures) → easier sync
- Chaplin's Tramp stays silent — a language everyone understands

## Reading — "The Long History Behind 'Color Movies'", Christopher McKittrick, LiveAbout (updated June 21, 2019)
Pasted by teacher. Copyrighted: facts only, never its sentences.
- Myth: no clean break from B&W to color; color existed far earlier than people think. B&W later an artistic choice: Young Frankenstein (1974), Manhattan (1979), Raging Bull (1980), Schindler's List (1993), The Artist (2011)
- Myth: The Wizard of Oz (1939) first color film — wrong by 35+ years; Oz uses color symbolically after a B&W (sepia) opening
- Tinting: dye whole scenes (night = blue/purple) — a representation of color, not real color
- Stenciling: hand-color each frame — A Trip to the Moon (1902), Vie et Passion du Christ (1903); slow, costly, rare
- Kinemacolor: George Albert Smith (England) 1906; red + green filters; two colors miss/wash out others; first film A Visit to the Seaside (1908); popular in UK, equipment too costly for many theaters
- Technicolor two-color 1917 The Gulf Between, first U.S. color feature; two projectors (red, green) + prism; only film in that process
- Handschiegl color process (Max Handschiegl, Famous Players-Lasky → Paramount), dyes; debut Cecil B. DeMille's Joan the Woman (1917); limited use ~10 years; dye tech used later
- Early 1920s Technicolor: color printed on the film itself → any projector (cf. earlier Prizma); The Toll of the Sea (1922); costly, needs more light → used for short sequences, e.g. Phantom of the Opera (1925)
- 1932 three-color dye-transfer Technicolor: debut Disney's Flowers and Trees (Disney exclusive deal); The Cat and the Fiddle (1934) live-action use [fact-check: it had a three-strip finale; Becky Sharp (1935) was the first full feature in three-strip]
- Big rented cameras, costly → color for prestige pictures late 1930s–1950s
- 1950s Eastmancolor (Kodak) rivals Technicolor, works with widescreen CinemaScope; color + widescreen fight TV's small B&W screens
- Late 1950s most Hollywood films in color; by mid-1960s B&W = artistic choice (indie films); digital now makes color film processes nearly obsolete

## Film Language — student vocabulary worksheet (teacher pasted)
Students define each term in their own words from three glossaries: filmsite.org filmterms (Tim Dirks' AMC Filmsite, labelled "IMDB glossary"),
University of West Georgia glossary of film terms, NYFA glossary. These terms = the Film Language section's vocabulary.
My own-words working definitions (answer key for questions; check against the glossaries before writing):
A. Sequence / scene — scene: action in one place and time; sequence: a run of scenes forming one unit of the story (a chase, a wedding)
B. Cut — an instant switch from one shot to the next; also the editor's act of joining shots
C. Shot — one unbroken run of the camera, from when it starts rolling until it stops or is cut away
 1. Establishing / wide / long shot — shows the whole place from far off, so viewers know where (and often when) the scene happens; people look small
 2. Medium shot — a person from about the waist up; shows body language and a little background
 3. Close shot / close-up — fills the frame with a face or object; shows emotion or an important detail
 4. POV (omniscient) — the camera as an all-seeing observer outside any character, showing what no single character could
 5. POV (subjective) — the camera shows exactly what one character sees, as if through their eyes
 6. Still — a single photograph from or of a film (a production still / frame); no motion
 7. Over the shoulder (OTS) — framed from behind one person's shoulder looking at another; standard for conversations
 8. Zoom — the lens changes focal length so the image grows or shrinks while the camera stays put
D. Angles
 1. High angle (bird's eye / aerial) — camera looks down on the subject; makes it seem small, weak or trapped; aerial = from far overhead
 2. Low angle (worm's eye) — camera looks up at the subject; makes it seem big, powerful or threatening
 3. Wide angle — a lens with a wide field of view: takes in much of the scene, deep focus, can stretch distances and bend edges
 4. Dutch angle — camera tilted so the horizon slants; signals unease, madness, something wrong
E. Moving camera
 1. Tilt — camera pivots up or down on a fixed base
 2. Pan — camera pivots left or right on a fixed base
 3. Tracking / following shot — the camera travels alongside or behind a moving subject (often on rails/tracks)
 4. Dolly / truck — dolly: a wheeled cart the camera rides on (dolly in/out = toward/away); truck: moving the camera sideways
 5. Boom shot — the camera rises or sinks through the air on a long arm (crane), often to reveal a scene from above
 6. Boom — the long pole or arm itself; also the pole that holds a microphone over actors just outside the frame
F. Transitions
 1. Fade out — picture gradually darkens to black; ends a scene or marks time passing
 2. Fade in — picture gradually appears from black; opens a scene
 3. Dissolve — one shot fades out while the next fades in, so they briefly overlap; suggests time passing or a link between the two
 4. Wipe — the new shot pushes across the screen, replacing the old one along a moving edge
 5. Freeze / freeze frame — the action stops on a single frame that holds on screen
 6. Jump cut — a cut within one shot or between nearly identical shots, so time visibly skips and the subject seems to jump
 7. Smash cut — a sudden, jarring cut, often from loud to quiet or calm to chaos, for shock or comedy

## GENRE: Film Noir — teacher links
- nofilmschool.com/film-noir-meaning; nofilmschool.com/2015/07/stylistic-elements-film-noir-explained-one-handy-infographic (BFI "Darkness Visible" infographic, Adam Frost / Melanie Patrick);
  filmnoirfoundation.org (nonprofit, Eddie Muller; preserves/restores noir, 35mm prints, NOIR CITY festival/magazine; calls noir "an international cinematic movement");
  studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-film-noir/
Copyrighted: facts and ideas only; no quoted film dialogue beyond a few words.
- Term: French "black/dark film"; coined by French critics after WWII (1946, Nino Frank) for the US crime films they'd missed during the war
- Classic era: early 1940s (The Maltese Falcon 1941) to late 1950s (Touch of Evil 1958 often called the end)
- Roots: German Expressionism (The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari; chiaroscuro, distorted sets, psychology); émigré directors fleeing the Nazis — Fritz Lang, Billy Wilder, Robert Siodmak; American hardboiled pulp fiction — Hammett, Chandler, James M. Cain
- Mood/context: Depression cynicism, WWII trauma, postwar disillusionment, changing gender roles; pessimism, fatalism
- Hays Code (Production Code, 1930–68) limited sex and violence → tension through lighting and composition instead
- BFI checklist of noir elements: investigator of relative integrity (Sam Spade, Philip Marlowe, Jeff Bailey); criminal, usually a murderer (Walter Neff, Kasper Gutman, Eddie Mars); Woman 1 bad and beautiful = femme fatale (Brigid O'Shaughnessy, Kitty Collins, Kathie Moffat); Woman 2 good and bland (Ann Grayle, Ann Miller, Katie Bannion); stolen money or valuables (the Maltese falcon, a jade necklace, a bank robbery); dark oppressive lighting (Wilder: rushes so dark you couldn't see); script from American pulp fiction; heavy smoking/drinking; obsession with the past — flashbacks (Double Indemnity, Detour, Murder My Sweet); complex/far-fetched plot (Out of the Past); urban location (LA, San Francisco); bleak view of humanity, rotten and corrupt; bleak downbeat ending; quick-fire poetic dialogue; poster with startling tagline; technically ingenious direction
- Breaking classic Hollywood's rules (classic → noir): balanced → asymmetrical composition; foreground focus → deep focus; low contrast with fill light → high contrast, no fill, long shadows; avoid confusing → mirrors and reflections for drama/unreality; invisible eye-level camera → extreme high and low angles; not too close → "choker" close-ups; also wide-angle lenses, Dutch tilt, diagonal bands of shadow (bars, staircases, venetian blinds), disruptive cuts, obscured vision (smoke, rain, fog, drugged/knocked-out hero)
- Narration: first-person voiceover, past tense, fatalistic; non-linear, flashbacks; double-crosses; MacGuffin (the falcon statue)
- "Noirest" film by the BFI checklist: Double Indemnity (1944) — the only one with ALL elements. Critics' top (12 lists): Double Indemnity, Out of the Past, Touch of Evil (10 each); The Big Sleep, In a Lonely Place, The Maltese Falcon (8); Laura 7
- Key films: The Maltese Falcon (1941, Bogart), Double Indemnity (1944, Wilder), Laura (1944), Murder, My Sweet (1944), Detour (1945, B-noir), The Big Sleep (1946), The Killers (1946), Gilda (1946), The Blue Dahlia (1946), Out of the Past (1947), The Third Man (1949, British), Sunset Boulevard (1950), In a Lonely Place (1950), Gun Crazy (1950), Pickup on South Street (1953), Kiss Me Deadly (1955), Touch of Evil (1958, Welles)
- B-noirs: low budgets → room to experiment
- Neo-noir: Chinatown (1974), Blade Runner (1982), Se7en (1995), L.A. Confidential (1997), Memento (2000), Mulholland Drive (2001)
- Links to other sections: Dutch angle, high/low angles, close-ups, wide-angle (Film Language); Golden Age studio system + Hays Code; B&W as a choice (color section)
